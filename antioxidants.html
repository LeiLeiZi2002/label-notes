Chapter 02 / Antioxidants

Step 01

Start here

Before you begin · No score

Day one on the snack line

It’s your first day as a quality-control intern at Crunch Line Snacks. Four product lines, four problems on the whiteboard. Your supervisor hands you a marker: “Take a guess before you read the reports.”

A cut apple can turn brown. Oils in cereal or crackers can develop rancid flavors. This chapter looks at the ingredients that help slow these changes.

What is the ingredient protecting?

Start with the food. An antioxidant used on a cut apple protects the exposed fruit surface; one used in crackers may protect the oils. As you read each label, connect the ingredient name to its job in that food.

You will meet these ingredients on real packages, follow a worked example, then try reading labels yourself.

Four terms you’ll see in this chapter

Oxidation: a reaction that can change food

Oils can react with oxygen during storage. That can change how they smell or taste.

Rancidity: off smells and flavors in fats and oils

Oxidation is one way fats and oils can develop stale or unpleasant flavors.

Antioxidant: something that slows oxidation

An antioxidant can help oil keep its flavor or help a cut fruit keep its color.

Enzymatic browning: what happens to a cut apple

When you cut an apple, enzymes in the fruit can react with compounds in the presence of oxygen. The cut surface turns brown.

Step 02

Ingredients & their uses

Antioxidants help slow changes in a food’s color, flavor and smell. Meet the ingredients used in fruit, cereal, crackers and other everyday foods.

Manufacturer’s product information

01Ascorbic acid

Vitamin C; helps slow browning.

Cut an apple and the exposed flesh gradually turns brown. Oxygen takes part in this change. Ascorbic acid helps interrupt the browning process, keeping cut fruit looking fresh for longer.

On this bag of apple slices, ascorbic acid is part of the mixture used to maintain freshness and color. You may also see the name followed by “vitamin C” on fruit drinks and fruit sauces.

Why is vitamin C called an antioxidant?

An antioxidant slows oxidation: reactions that can change a food’s color, flavor or smell. Ascorbic acid can react before other food components do, helping protect them.

02Calcium ascorbate

A form of vitamin C used to protect fresh-cut fruit.

Calcium ascorbate is the calcium salt of ascorbic acid. It also helps slow browning. In these apple slices, it works alongside ascorbic acid to help the fruit retain its color.

The ascorbate part of the name connects it to ascorbic acid. Calcium ascorbate and calcium carbonate are different ingredients, even though both names begin with “calcium.”

What does “salt” mean here?

A salt is a type of chemical compound. Here, calcium is paired with ascorbate. The word does not mean that table salt was added to vitamin C.

Ingredient list as text

Apples (apples, calcium ascorbate, ascorbic acid, calcium carbonate [to maintain freshness and color]).

03Mixed tocopherols

Helps keep fats from developing off-flavors.

Tocopherols belong to the vitamin E family. They dissolve in fats and help slow the reactions that make oils and fat-containing foods develop stale, rancid flavors.

This cereal gives the purpose right beside the name: “Added to Preserve Freshness.” Its grains contain small amounts of fat that can oxidize during storage. Tocopherols help protect those fats.

What does “mixed” mean?

There are several forms of tocopherol, including alpha, beta, gamma and delta. “Mixed tocopherols” means that more than one form is present.

04Sodium ascorbate

A vitamin C ingredient with antioxidant uses.

Sodium ascorbate is the sodium salt of ascorbic acid. Like calcium ascorbate, it belongs to the vitamin C family. It can help protect foods against oxidation and can also be used to add vitamin C.

Here it appears in the cereal’s vitamins and minerals list as vitamin C. The explicit freshness statement belongs to mixed tocopherols. The two names play different roles on this label.

Vitamins and Minerals: Calcium Carbonate, Iron and Zinc (mineral nutrients), A B Vitamin (niacinamide), Vitamin C (sodium ascorbate), A B Vitamin (calcium pantothenate), Vitamin B6 (pyridoxine hydrochloride), Vitamin B2 (riboflavin), Vitamin B1 (thiamin mononitrate), A B Vitamin (folic acid), Vitamin B12, Vitamin A (palmitate), Vitamin D3.

05Rosemary extract

Helps protect oils and fats from oxidation.

Rosemary extract contains antioxidant compounds taken from rosemary leaves. These compounds help slow the reactions that give fats an unpleasant taste or smell.

These crackers contain sunflower oil, almonds and seeds. Their label lists rosemary extract “for freshness,” so its stated job is to help preserve the crackers rather than give them a rosemary flavor.

Is this the same as rosemary seasoning?

The seasoning is the herb itself. An extract concentrates selected compounds from the leaves. Carnosic acid and carnosol are two compounds responsible for rosemary extract’s antioxidant activity.

Ingredient list as text

Nut & Seed Flour Blend (almonds, sunflower seeds, flax seeds), Tapioca Starch, Cassava Flour, Organic Sunflower Oil, Sea Salt, Organic Onion, Organic Garlic, Rosemary Extract (for freshness). Contains: Almonds.

06TBHQ

An antioxidant used to protect fats and oils.

TBHQ is short for tert-butylhydroquinone. It slows fat oxidation, helping foods made with oil keep their flavor during storage.

In this ingredient list, “preserved by TBHQ” follows the vegetable oil used in the noodles. That wording tells you what is being protected. TBHQ is not what gives the soup its chicken flavor.

What is rancidity?

Rancidity is the development of unpleasant flavors and smells as fats break down. Oxidation is one cause. Antioxidants help delay this change; they do not restore oil that has already gone rancid.

Ingredient list as text

Ramen noodle ingredients: Enriched wheat flour (wheat flour, niacin, reduced iron, thiamine mononitrate, riboflavin, folic acid), vegetable oil (contains one or more of the following: canola, cottonseed, palm) preserved by TBHQ, contains less than 1% of: salt, potassium carbonate, soy sauce (water, wheat, soybeans, salt), sodium carbonate, sodium hexametaphosphate, sodium tripolyphosphate, monosodium phosphate.

Contains wheat, soy, and milk ingredients. Manufactured in a facility that also processes crustacean shellfish and sesame products.

07BHA

Helps preserve the flavor of fatty foods.

Butylated hydroxyanisole, usually shortened to BHA, slows oxidation in fats. It is used in foods such as cured meats and some dry foods to help prevent off-flavors during storage.

This pepperoni’s ingredient list includes BHA alongside BHT and citric acid. The antioxidants help protect the fat in the meat.

08BHT

Another antioxidant used to slow fat oxidation.

BHT stands for butylated hydroxytoluene. Like BHA, it helps delay the development of rancid flavors in foods containing fats and oils.

BHA and BHT are two different substances, despite their similar abbreviations. A recipe may use them together, as this 3.5 oz pepperoni listing does.

Ingredient list as text

Pork, beef, salt, contains 2% or less of water, dextrose, spices, lactic acid starter culture, oleoresin of paprika, garlic powder, sodium nitrite, BHA, BHT, citric acid.

09Sodium erythorbate

Helps develop and maintain cured-meat color.

Sodium erythorbate is an antioxidant commonly used in cured meat. It speeds up the curing reaction and helps keep the meat’s characteristic color. It also helps protect against oxidation.

You met it beside sodium nitrite in Chapter 01. They do different jobs: nitrite contributes to curing, while erythorbate helps the curing process and color stability.

Erythorbic acid and sodium erythorbate

Sodium erythorbate is the sodium salt of erythorbic acid. Erythorbic acid is related in structure to ascorbic acid, but erythorbate should not be read as a vitamin C nutrition claim.

Ingredient list as text

Cured with water, salt, sugar, dextrose, sodium erythorbate, sodium nitrite. Keep refrigerated.

Two more names you may come across

10Ascorbyl palmitate

A vitamin C derivative used to protect fats.

Ascorbyl palmitate combines ascorbic acid with palmitic acid, a fatty acid. This makes it more suited to fatty foods than plain ascorbic acid. It is used to help slow oxidation in oils and other fat-containing products.

Reading the name

“Ascorbyl” points to the ascorbic-acid part; “palmitate” points to the fatty-acid part. It is a different ingredient from sodium or calcium ascorbate.

11Propyl gallate

Helps delay oxidation in fats and oils.

Propyl gallate is made from gallic acid and propyl alcohol. As an antioxidant, it helps protect fats from developing rancid flavors. It can be used in antioxidant mixtures alongside other ingredients.

FDA: ascorbyl palmitate · National Library of Medicine: propyl gallate

Citric acid and EDTA

Citric acid and EDTA can support antioxidant protection by controlling acidity or binding metals. Their roles differ from those of the antioxidants above.

12Citric acid

Helps control acidity and supports antioxidant protection.

Citric acid is the acid associated with the tart taste of citrus fruit. In foods it can adjust acidity and bind small amounts of metals that would otherwise speed up oxidation.

It is often used alongside antioxidants, including BHA and BHT in this pepperoni. In other products it may mainly add tartness or adjust acidity. Its job depends on the food.

Citric acid is not vitamin C

Vitamin C is ascorbic acid. Citric acid and ascorbic acid can both help protect fruit color, but they are different ingredients and work in different ways.

Ingredient list as text

Pork, beef, salt, contains 2% or less of water, dextrose, spices, lactic acid starter culture, oleoresin of paprika, garlic powder, sodium nitrite, BHA, BHT, citric acid.

13Calcium disodium EDTA

Binds metals that can speed up flavor changes.

Tiny amounts of metals such as iron and copper can speed up oxidation. Calcium disodium EDTA binds these metals, making them less able to drive those reactions.

Mayonnaise contains a large amount of oil. This ingredient helps protect its flavor and quality during storage. It supports preservation by binding metals, rather than working in the same way as TBHQ or tocopherols.

What does EDTA stand for?

EDTA stands for ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid. You may see its metal-binding role called “chelating” or “sequestering.” Calcium disodium EDTA is one form used in foods.

Ingredient list as text

Soybean oil, water, whole eggs, distilled vinegar, egg yolks, salt, sugar, lemon juice concentrate, calcium disodium EDTA (used to protect quality), natural flavors. Contains egg.

A common mix-up: “Citric acid is vitamin C.”

Vitamin C is ascorbic acid. Citric acid is a different substance: it can adjust acidity and bind metals that contribute to oxidation. Similar-looking names do not make the functions identical.

A vitamin name can have two jobs

Ascorbic acid and tocopherols are vitamin-related ingredients, but a manufacturer may use them for antioxidant protection, nutrient addition, or both. Start with the nearby wording.

On the cereal shown here, the freshness statement follows mixed tocopherols. Sodium ascorbate appears in the Vitamins and Minerals group. Nutrition Facts describes nutrient amounts in the food; it does not, on its own, explain why every ingredient was added. If the label gives no purpose, the name alone may leave the intended use uncertain.

Step 03

Read a food label

Four steps for reading a label

Worked example · follow the evidence, one step at a time.

  1. Find the purpose statement.

    Find “preserved by TBHQ” beside the vegetable oil.

  2. Read the name and its family.

    Look closely at the letters. TBHQ is not the same additive as BHA or BHT.

  3. Connect it to this food.

    Connect the purpose to the oil: slowing oxidation helps delay rancid flavors.

  4. Notice what the label does not say.

    This line gives a food-preservation purpose. It does not establish a health benefit for the person eating it.

Step 04

Review & practice

“No artificial preservatives” is a limited claim

That wording does not mean a food has no ingredients helping it keep. A recipe may use rosemary extract or tocopherols for antioxidant protection. Read the ingredient list and any purpose statement to see what the ingredient is doing.

A source claim does not establish a health benefit, unlimited shelf life or freedom from storage instructions. The crackers in this chapter provide a concrete clue: rosemary extract is listed “for freshness.” We are using that wording to explain its function, not claiming that this package carries every example claim discussed here.

Sources
  • eCFR: ingredient names and preservative functions
  • FDA: antioxidant ingredients

A common mix-up: “An antioxidant makes the whole food healthier.”

Preserving the food during storage and demonstrating a benefit for the person eating it are different claims. The freshness wording provides evidence for the first.
