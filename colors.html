Chapter 05 / Food color additives

Step 01

Start here

Two bags of candy, two ways to color the rainbow

Set a standard pouch of Skittles beside a "No FD&C Colors" pouch. Both look bright red, yellow, green, and purple, yet their ingredient lists read completely differently. One lists numbered synthetic colors like Red 40 Lake and Blue 1. The other relies on plant and mineral sources such as beet juice concentrate, turmeric, and spirulina extract.

Under U.S. FDA rules, food colors fall into two legal groups: certified synthetic colors (tested batch by batch) and exempt colors derived from plants, minerals, or animals.

Four terms you'll see in this chapter
Certified color (FD&C)
A synthetically produced color tested by the FDA batch by batch and listed on U.S. labels by color name and number (such as Red 40 or Blue 1).
Exempt color
A color derived from natural sources—plants, minerals, or animals—that does not require FDA batch certification.
Dye
A water-soluble certified color that dissolves cleanly in clear drinks, syrups, and watery foods.
Lake
A water-insoluble form of a certified color bound to a substrate, used in candy shells and fat-rich coatings so colors do not bleed.

04FD&C Lakes (Red 40 Lake, Blue 1 Lake, Yellow 5 Lake)

Skittles, M&M's

What it does · Water-insoluble pigments made by binding a dye to an inert base. They color hard candy shells, fat-rich frostings, and dry mixes without bleeding onto fingers or into neighboring layers.

Part B · Exempt-from-Certification Colors

Why some labels just say "Color added"

Certified synthetic colors must be named individually (such as Red 40 or Yellow 5). Many exempt natural-source colors may be listed either by their specific name (like annatto or beet juice) or simply as "color added"—with strict exceptions such as carmine, which must always be named explicitly.

Step 03

Read a food label

Read the Goldfish Cheddar label. One color sits inside another ingredient’s list.

Four steps for reading a label

  1. Read inside the parentheses.

    Follow the cheddar cheese entry to its own ingredient list and find annatto.

  2. Look for other color names.

    Paprika also appears among the ingredients used in small amounts.

  3. Separate color from flavor.

    Autolyzed yeast extract boosts the savory taste. Annatto contributes color.

  4. Check the front against the list.

    Goldfish says there are no artificial colors. Annatto and paprika still contribute to the crackers’ color.

Step 04

Review & practice

A common mix-up: "No Artificial Colors" means no color was added.

Front-panel claims like "No Artificial Colors" or "No FD&C Colors" rule out certified synthetic dyes, not exempt colors like annatto, paprika, beet juice, or carmine.

FDA: “No Artificial Colors” labeling claims · Skittles: Colors by formula

A common mix-up: "Natural-source colors are always plant-based and allergy-free."

Exempt colors include insect-derived carmine (which can trigger rare allergic reactions and is not vegan) and seed extracts like annatto. "Exempt from certification" describes regulatory category, not universal suitability.
